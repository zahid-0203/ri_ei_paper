	\begin{abstract}
	\zahid{tbd}
	Deep neural networks often achieve high classification accuracy. Despite high accuracy, the reliability of CNN models remains a concern. Sometimes CNN models may rely on background or irrelevant features, undermining trustworthiness. Besides, with the use of perturbations, models' accuracy and object reliance may decline. Such degradation raises questions about their robustness. In this research, we have proposed two novel evaluation metrics: the \textbf{Explainable Trustworthy Index (ETI)} and the \textbf{Explainable Robustness Index (ERI)}. ETI combines accuracy with the \textbf{Localization Mask Overlap Score (LMOS)}, which measures the proportion of Grad-CAM salient pixels that fall within the ground-truth object regions. A model with high ETI clearly indicates that both its accuracy and object reliance are high and satisfactory. ERI extends this to perturbed inputs by jointly evaluating accuracy stability and LMOS stability.  Like ETI, a model with high ERI indicates that both its accuracy and object focus remain stable under the use of perturbations. We have used eight CNN architectures on four datasets and nine perturbations (e.g., noise, blur, motion blur, occlusion) to calculate ETI and ERI. We have demonstrated that high accuracy sometimes does not guarantee high ETI or ERI. For instance, on the EPLI dataset, Custom CNN has achieved 99.45\% accuracy but the lowest ETI (0.5922) due to poor object localization. It has also provided the lowest ERI. ETI and ERI provide a more holistic assessment of model reliability for safety-critical applications.
	\end{abstract}

	Prior literature has established diverse methodologies for evaluating the reliability, robustness, and explanation quality of convolutional neural networks (CNNs). 
	Specifically, reliability has been assessed via uncertainty estimation \cite{}, calibration \cite{}, confidence scoring \cite{}, failure detection \cite{}, risk-coverage trade-offs \cite{}, and broader trustworthiness frameworks \cite{}. 
	Robustness is typically evaluated by measuring performance degradation under common corruptions \cite{}, natural distribution shifts \cite{}, and targeted input perturbations \cite{}, with recent efforts also examining how corruptions affect explanation stability \cite{}. 
	Concurrently, explanation quality and visual feature reliance have been measured using faithfulness metrics \cite{}, ground-truth alignment \cite{}, and the spatial overlap between visual explanations and target object regions \cite{}.
	
	Despite these advances, existing evaluation methods typically focus on isolated dimensions. 
	%Existing methods lack a unified, model-level evaluation framework that simultaneously accounts for predictive accuracy, the spatial fidelity of decision-relevant visual evidence, and the joint stability of both (accuracy and spatial fidelity) under input perturbations.

	Conversely, its underlying visual focus may shift drastically even when accuracy appears invariant. 
	To address these limitations, a comprehensive evaluation framework is needed to jointly assess predictive performance, object-centric explanation quality, and explanation-aware robustness.

	The proposed ERI combines \emph{accuracy stability} and \emph{LMOS stability} to provide a joint measure of explainable robustness.

	\section{Problem Definition}
	\label{sec:problem_def}
	Let  $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i,M_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a dataset containing $N$ test images, where $x_i\in \mathbb R^{n\times m}$ is the $i$-th image, $y_i$ is its ground-truth label, and $M_i$ is the corresponding ground-truth mask of the target object. 
	%Let $\mathcal{F}$ denote a set of $K$ CNN architectures evaluated on the same test dataset. Here, $\mathcal{F} = \{f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_K\}$. 
	Conventional evaluation methods primarily measure the classification accuracy of a model. 
	Let $acc\in[0,1]$ denote the classification accuracy of a model. 
	
	%Although acc measures predictive performance, it does not indicate whether the model's decision-relevant activation is spatially aligned with the target object. 
	Two CNNs may achieve identical accuracy while relying on substantially different image regions. 
	In particular, a model may achieve high accuracy while its Grad-CAM activation map reveals that the activated regions concentrated outside the target object. 
	Hence, accuracy is insufficient to distinguish models according to their object-focused explanatory behavior.
	
	%We want to quantify the aspect concerning a model's ratianale behind the predictions. 
	%Let $H_{i}$ denotes the Grad-CAM heatmap corresponding to image $x_i$ and prediction $\hat y_i$.

	We seek to quantify the alignment between a model's rationale and its predictions. 
	Let $H_i$ denote the Grad-CAM heatmap corresponding to image $x_i$ and prediction $\hat{y}_i$. 
	We define a scoring function $\mathrm{LMOS}_i \in [0, 1]$ to measure the overlap ratio between the decision-relevant regions of $H_i$ and the ground-truth object mask $M_i$ %and $ L$ is the mean LMOS value across all images.
	%and the total highlighted regions, \(H_{i}\). 
	To indicate model trustworthiness and robustness, we address the following two sub problems in this paper.
	
	Firstly, we want to evaluate CNN models considering both their accuracy ($acc$) and mean object-focused explanation quality ($\mathrm{LMOS}$). 
	Formally, the objective is to develop an evaluation measure $T$ as a function of $acc$ and $\mathrm{LMOS}$: $T = \Psi(acc, \mathrm{LMOS})$. 
	We address this problem by the proposed Explainable Trustworthy Index (ETI).
	
	Secondly, we want to incorporate CNNs behavioural changes in response to perturbations in model evaluation metric. 
	Let $\mathcal{P}=\{p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_P\}$ denote a set of input perturbations. 
	A robust CNN should not only preserve its predictive performance under any changes in $\mathcal{P}$ but also maintain a similar object-focused activation pattern simailar to the model's behavior on the corresponding clean image. 
	%Therefore, accuracy stability and explanation stability need to be considered jointly.
	%Let $acc_{\mathrm{clean}}$ and $acc_{\mathrm{perturbed}}$ denote the classification accuracy of a model on the clean and perturbed test sets, respectively.

	%The perturbation-specific accuracy stability and LMOS stability are therefore defined as measures of how closely the model preserves its predictive performance and object-focused behavior relative to the clean condition. 
	Let $S_{\mathrm{acc}}$ and $S _ {\mathrm{LMOS}}$ represent the overall accuracy stability (the model's stability to perform under purturbation) and LMOS stability (the model's stability to remain the object-focused for making predictions) of a model. 
	Our second problem is to obtain an evaluation measure $R$ that jointly captures these two forms of stability across the evaluated perturbations: $R = \Phi(S_{\mathrm{acc}},  S_{\mathrm{LMOS}})$. 
	We propose Explainable Robustness Index (ERI) to address this problem.

	\section{Explainable Trustworthy Index and Explainable Robustness Index}
	\label{sec:proposed_method}
	Both of the proposed Explainable Trustworthy Index (ETI) and Explainable Robustness Index (ERI) metrics use Localization Mask Overlap Score (LMOS) that measures the consistency between model-generated explanations and ground-truth object regions. 
	ETI assesses if predictions are grounded in actual object regions rather than background noise. 
	ERI extends this framework to perturbed inputs, measuring joint predictive and localization stability.
	%We briefly describe the implementation details of these metrics below. 
	
	%\noindent
	\subsection{Localization Mask Overlap Score (LMOS)} 
	\label{sec:lmos}
	LMOS calculates how much a model focuses on true object regions (instead of backgrounds) for prediction. 
	Let $H \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w}$ denote the Grad-CAM heatmap generated from the last convolutional layer of a network. 
	We obtain a binary attention map $\hat H (i,j)$ from $H$ by thresholding:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:binary_attention_map}
		\hat H (i,j) = \mathds{1}\bigl( H(i,j) > \tau \bigr)
	\end{equation}
	where $ \mathds{1}(\cdot)$ is an indicator function and $\tau$ is the threshold. 
	We set $\tau=0.7$ (see Section \ref{sec:justification_of_tau}) to restrict our LMOS calculations to red and yellow pixels, as the red and yelow pixels exhibit the highest activation levels in model predictions. 
	Hence, $\hat{H}(i,j)$ is a binary mask representing the activated decision-relevant regions for the corresponding predictions and input image. 
	
	We define LMOS as the ratio of activated decision-relevant regions inside the object region and the total activated decision-relevant regions. 
	Mathematically:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:lmos}
		\mathrm{LMOS}  = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{h}\sum_{j=1}^{w} \hat{H}(i,j)\, M(i,j)} {\sum_{i=1}^{h}\sum_{j=1}^{w} \hat{H}(i,j)}
	\end{equation}
	where $M \in \{0,1\}^{h \times w}$ denote the ground-truth binary segmentation mask.  
	
	\subsection{Explainable Trustworthy Index (ETI)}
	\label{sec:implementaiton_TI}
	We propose Explainable Trustworthy Index (ETI) to measure trustworthiness of a CNN model's predictions. 
	We combine the accuracy, explainable consistency and the mean LMOS (Eq. \ref{eq:lmos}) to define ETI. 
	Note that the LMOS is an instance specific (or local) measure. 
	On the other hand accuray is a global measure indicating the overall performance of a model. 
	%Thats why we use $\overline{LMOS}$ in TI definition, just need to say it outloud here}
	
	Let $acc$ denote the classification accuracy of a model on test set and $\overline{\mathrm{LMOS}}$ denote the mean LMOS scores across the same test samples. 
	We define ETI as follows:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:eti}
		\mathrm{ETI} = \frac{acc + \overline{\mathrm{LMOS}}}{2}.
	\end{equation}
	
	In this study, both $acc$ and $\mathrm{LMOS}$ are equally important and are normalized to the range $[0,1]$.
	% and represent complementary components of model reliability. 
	Here, $acc$ measures the overall predictive performance of a model, whereas $\mathrm{LMOS}$ measures the mean explanation consistency specific to the target object. 
	%Since neither component alone is sufficient to estimate trustworthiness, 
	We assign equal weighting to both components to avoid favoring either predictive performance or explanatory quality.
	
	\subsection{Explainable Robustness Index (ERI)}
	We estimate the robustness of a model by measuring its' performance deviation under purturbation.  
	%To evaluate performance robustness against input perturbations, we compute Accuracy Stability ($S_{\rm{acc}}$)  \zahid{if not introduced by you cite}, which measures the consistency between the classification accuracy on perturbed images and the baseline accuracy on clean test images.
	Let $acc_c$ be the accuracy of a model on a clean dataset (not purturbed) and $acc_p$ denote the accuracy obtained on the same dataset after applying a specific perturbation $p$ (see Sec. \ref{sec:perturbation_methods} for all the purturbation methods used in this research). 
	We intorduce the Accuracy Stability ($S_{\rm{acc}}$) score \zahid{if not introduced by you cite} under a specific perturbation $p$ as:
	%begin{equation}
	$$	S_{\mathrm{acc}}^{p} = 1 - \frac{ | acc_c - acc_p | }
	{acc_c + acc_p + \epsilon}$$
	%\end{equation}
	where $\epsilon$ is a small positive constant introduced to avoid division by zero error. 
	%A higher value of $S_{\mathrm{acc}}^p$ indicates that the model's performance is more consistent under the corresponding perturbed condition $p$. 
	A higher $S_{\mathrm{acc}}^p$ value indicates greater performance consistency under the perturbed condition $p$.
	
	To estimate an overall measure of accuracy stability ($S_{\mathrm{acc}}$), we compute $S_{\mathrm{acc}}^p$ across all $p \in \mathcal P$ where $\mathcal P$ is a set of purturbation methods, see Section \ref{sec:perturbation_methods}, and take the mean of all $S_{\mathrm{acc}}^p$. 
	We define this as follows:
	%\begin{equation}
	$$S_{\text{acc}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal P|} \sum_{p=1}^{|\mathcal P|} S_{\text{acc}}^{p}$$
	%\end{equation}
	%where $S_{\text{acc}}^{(p)}$ denotes the accuracy stability under the $p$-th perturbation and $P$ is the total %number of evaluated perturbations. In this study, $P=9$.
	
	%\zahid{proposed measures EI and RI. they use LMOS. What are the additional measures - stability ??? Need to clarify.}
	
	Similarly, we measure LMOS stability ($S_{\mathrm{LMOS}}$) to quantify how consistently a model maintains its visual object focus under input perturbations relative to unperturbed images.
	%Similarly, we computer LMOS Stability to measure how closely a model's object reliance under perturbation remains to its object reliance on clean images. 
	%To evaluate the robustness under input perturbations, we have measured the stability of LMOS when input images are perturbed.
	For each sample in a dataset, we comput LMOS for both the original image and its perturbed version using Eq. \ref{eq:lmos}.  
	
	%$N$ be the number of samples in a dataset, 
	Let $\mathrm{LMOS}^i_c$ be the LMOS value computed from sample $i$ and $\mathrm{LMOS}^i_p$ be the LMOS value computed from the same sample $i$  after applying perturbation $p$. 
	To quantify the consistency between these two values, we define the LMOS stability under a perturbation $p$ for sample $i$ as:
	%\begin{equation}
	$$S^{p, i}_{\textrm{LMOS}} = 1 - \frac{|\mathrm{LMOS}^i_c - \mathrm{LMOS}^i_p|}{\mathrm{LMOS}^i_c + \mathrm{LMOS}^i_p + \epsilon}$$ 
	%\end{equation}
	where $\epsilon$ is a small constant to avoid division by zero error. 
	A higher value of $S^{p,i}_{\textrm{LMOS}}$ indicates that the model's reliance on the focus object remains more consistent under the corresponding perturbation. 

	Given there are $N$ images in a dataset, the LMOS stability under the perturbation $p$ across the entire dataset is estimated as:
	%\begin{equation}

	%To jointly evaluate the robustness of both classification performance and explainability under input perturbations, we have proposed the \textit{Explainable Robustness Index} (ERI).

	To jointly evaluate the robustness of predictive accuracy and explainability under input perturbations, we propose the \textit{Explainable Robustness Index} (ERI). 
	While accuracy stability and LMOS stability independently assess predictive consistency and visual localization behavior, ERI provides a unified robustness measure by integrating both dimensions.
	
	%Let ${s}_{\text{acc}}^{{p}}$ be accuracy stability and ${s}_{\text{LMOS}}^{{p}}$ be LMOS stability under perturbation p. Now, ERI under perturbation p is calculated as:
	
	%Let $S_{\mathrm{acc}}^{p}$ and $S_{\mathrm{LMOS}}^{p}$ denote the accuracy stability and LMOS stability under perturbation $p$, respectively. 
	
	The ERI under perturbation $p$ ($\mathrm{ERI}^p$) is then defined by combining the accuracy stability $S_{\mathrm{acc}}$ and LMOS stability $S_{\mathrm{LMOS}}$ as follows:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:eri}
		\mathrm{ERI} =\frac{S_{\mathrm{acc}}+
			S_{\mathrm{LMOS}}}{2}.
	\end{equation}
	
	Here, $S_{\mathrm{acc}}$ represents the overall consistency of classification accuracy between clean and perturbed inputs, while $S_{\mathrm{LMOS}}$ represents the overall consistency of the model's reliance on the focus object under perturbations. 
	ERI jointly captures two complementary dimensions of robustness: \textit{predictive stability} and \textit{explanatory stability}.
	
	%Since both component scores are bounded in the same range, the resulting ERI also provides a normalized measure of overall robustness. 
	%A higher ERI indicates that a model simultaneously maintains its classification performance and preserves its object-focused behavior across the evaluated perturbations. 
	%Conversely, a lower ERI indicates that the model exhibits greater instability in one or both dimensions.
	Since both constituent scores share a common upper and lower bound, the resulting ERI serves as a normalized metric of overall robustness. 
	A higher ERI value signifies that a model concurrently preserves both its classification accuracy and object-centric visual focus across input perturbations. 
	Conversely, a lower ERI reflects marked instability in either predictive performance, visual focus, or both.

	\section{Related Works}
    \label{related_works}
    
	\subsection{Model Reliability and Trustworthiness}
	To move beyond standard classification accuracy, recent studies have increasingly focused on quantifying model reliability through uncertainty estimation and calibration. 
    Javed et al. \citet{r22} integrated Bayesian Monte Carlo Dropout with nonparametric Conformal Prediction to address overconfidence in deep neural networks. 
    To better evaluate model performance under uncertainty, Ao et al. \cite{r23} introduced the Excess Area Under the Optimal Risk-Coverage Curve ($\mathrm{E\text{-}AUoptRC}$) and the Trust Index ($\mathrm{TI}$), offering a risk-coverage perspective on model trustworthiness. 

	At a broader level, Nastoska et al. \cite{r24} provided a comprehensive review of AI trustworthiness criteria by evaluating industrial compliance against the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC standards. 
	In domain-specific applications, Pal et al. \cite{r25} proposed the Trust-Aware Explainable Artificial Intelligence ($\mathrm{TAXAI}$) framework, which yields a normalized score by combining algorithmic transparency, interpretability alignment, and compliance reliability. 
	While these frameworks advance the quantification of predictive trust, they generally evaluate reliability without explicitly measuring the spatial fidelity or stability of visual explanations under environmental perturbations.

	\subsection{Predictive Robustness Under Perturbations}
	Evaluating model performance under distribution shifts and input corruptions is essential for reliable deployment. 
	Hendrycks and Dietterich \cite{r27} established foundational benchmarks known as ImageNet-C and ImageNet-P, to measure classification degradation under common corruptions and perturbations. 
	Extending this, Schneider et al. \cite{r31} demonstrated that test-time covariate-shift adaptation can substantially recover predictive performance under corruptions, while Hendrycks et al. \cite{r30} proposed AugMix to improve model robustness while preserving clean accuracy. 
	Beyond common corruptions, Su et al. \cite{r28} and Taori et al. \cite{r29} investigated adversarial and natural distribution shifts, demonstrating that high standard accuracy does not guarantee robustness and advocating for independent robustness evaluations. 
	Furthermore, Guo et al. \cite{r32} compiled a 23-metric framework targeting adversarial data and model vulnerabilities.

	While these works establish rigorous protocols for evaluating accuracy under stress, they focus exclusively on output predictions. 
	A few recent efforts have attempted to incorporate explainability into robustness routines. 
	Zhang et al. \cite{r26} proposed $\mathrm{AR^2}$, aligning Class Activation Maps (CAMs) between clean and corrupted images during training to enforce feature consistency. 
	However, $\mathrm{AR^2}$ operates as a model repair mechanism rather than a quantitative evaluation index. 
	In contrast, our proposed Explainable Robustness Index ($\mathrm{ERI}$) directly quantifies the joint stability of both predictive performance and Grad-CAM visual evidence under input perturbations.

	\subsection{Explanation Quality, Localization, and Spurious Bias}
	Visual explanation techniques e.g., Grad-CAM, Attention maps, and Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) are widely deployed to interpret CNN decisions in sensitive domains, including smart farming and plant pathology \cite{r33, r34, r35}. 
	However, as highlighted by Shawon et al. \cite{r35}, high accuracy and visual heatmaps do not automatically guarantee model generalization under real-world variability. 
	A critical challenge remains the reliance on shortcut learning and background bias. 
	Bassi et al. \cite{r11} demonstrated that synthetic background biases heavily influence deep models, showing that LRP heatmaps can expose and mitigate shortcut learning without incurring high computational costs. 
	Similarly, Zheng et al. \cite{r39} introduced ShortcutProbe to detect and retrain networks against prediction shortcuts without manual annotations.
	
	A fundamental bottleneck in Explainable AI (XAI) is the scarcity of quantitative metrics for evaluating explanation quality. 
	Addressing this, Rahimiaghdam and Alemdar \cite{r36} formulated evaluative metrics for medical imaging, while Baniecki et al. \cite{r37} proposed aligning model explanation pipelines directly with ground-truth segmentation masks. 
	Ground-truth-based evaluation was further advanced by Guidotti et al. \cite{r40}, who evaluated local explanation methods against annotated object bounds. 
	Inspired by this perspective, our proposed Localization Mask Overlap Score ($\mathrm{LMOS}$) quantifies the spatial alignment between Grad-CAM visual evidence and target object regions. 
	
	Regarding explanation stability, Repetto et al. \cite{r38} systematically measured the sensitivity of XAI techniques under natural noise and adversarial manipulations that alter explanations without affecting predictions. 
	Finally, Fong and Vedaldi \cite{r41} and Kim et al. \cite{r42} analyzed black-box attribution and weakly supervised localization, observing that CAM-based methods often concentrate on localized, highly discriminative parts rather than full object extents. 
	Our framework builds upon these insights by offering a unified, quantitative evaluation of spatial overlap ($\mathrm{LMOS}$), explanation quality ($\mathrm{ETI}$), and perturbation stability ($\mathrm{ERI}$).

	
	\section{Conclusion}     
	\label{sec:conclusion}
	This paper has proposed two novel metrics, the Explainable Trustworthy Index (ETI) and the Explainable Robustness Index (ERI), to evaluate CNN models beyond classification accuracy. 
	ETI combines accuracy with the Localization Mask Overlap Score (LMOS) to assess whether a model's predictions are grounded in the intended object rather than background or irrelevant regions. 
	ERI extends this evaluation to perturbed inputs by jointly measuring accuracy stability and LMOS stability, capturing whether both predictive performance and object-focused reasoning remain consistent under real-world degradations.
	
	Across four benchmark datasets and eight CNN architectures, our results demonstrate that high classification accuracy does not guarantee high ETI or ERI. 
	For example, on the EPLI dataset, the CustomCNN model has achieved competitive or near-perfect accuracy while obtaining the lowest ETI and ERI scores due to poor and unstable object localization and its poor stability
	Similarly, the InceptionV3 achieves high classification accuracy across most conditions, its low ETI score reflects poor spatial focus on target object regions. 
	Conversely, Xception consistently has achieved the highest overall ERI across all four datasets and the highest ETI on both flower datasets (FIRS and FR), despite not always achieving the highest raw accuracy. 
	It illustrates that predictive performance and explanation quality/stability are related but distinct properties of model reliability. 
	MobileNetV2 has outperformed higher-accuracy models on the BDML dataset in terms of ETI, reinforcing this distinction.
	
	The cross-dataset comparison further shows that reliability patterns vary with visual domain: leaf datasets (EPLI and BDML) have generally yielded higher ETI than flower datasets (FLRS and FR), primarily due to lower LMOS on flowers, yet this does not translate into lower robustness. 
	Several models have attained high ERI on the flower datasets despite modest ETI. 
	This confirms that clean-image trustworthiness (ETI) and perturbation robustness (ERI) are complementary rather than interchangeable measures of reliability.
	
	Overall, ETI and ERI provide a more comprehensive framework for evaluating CNNs in safety-critical or trust-sensitive applications, where accuracy alone is an insufficient proxy for whether a model reasons about the correct visual evidence and whether that reasoning holds up under realistic input perturbations.
